For, during, while and still.

ACTIVITY 1 · GRAMMAR

1. FOR — expressing duration

Use "for" to indicate how long an action or situation lasts. It is followed by a period of time.

  • I studied for two hours.
  • She has lived here for five years.
  • They were waiting for thirty minutes.
  • We are going to stay for a week.

Common time expressions:

For ten minutes, for a long time, for several days, for two weeks.

FOR has more than one use

  • Duration: I waited for an hour.
  • Purpose or benefit: This book is for beginners.
  • Reason as FANBOYS: I left, for I was exhausted.

Always consider the sentence structure and meaning before choosing the correct use of "for".

2. DURING — within a period or event

Use "during" before a noun or noun phrase to say that something happened within a particular period or event.

  • Many students took notes during the lesson.
  • It rained during the night.
  • She received a phone call during the meeting.
  • He fell asleep during the film.

Structure: During + noun or noun phrase.

Correct: During the examination, nobody spoke.

Incorrect: During two hours, I studied.

For a duration of two hours, say: "I studied for two hours."

3. WHILE — at the same time

Use "while" to introduce a clause, often describing an action happening at the same time as another action.

  • While I was studying, my brother was cooking.
  • The teacher explained while the students were taking notes.
  • While the tiger was arguing, the donkey was becoming impatient.

Structure: While + subject + verb.

Compare:

During the lesson, I took notes.

While the teacher was speaking, I took notes.

"During" is followed by a noun phrase. "While" introduces a clause containing a subject and a verb.

4. STILL — emphasizing continuation

"Still" is an adverb. It emphasizes that a situation or action continues, often longer than expected.

  • She is still studying.
  • They still live in the same village.
  • He was still working when I arrived.
  • Are you still waiting?

Position of STILL

  • Before the main verb: She still works here.
  • After the verb "be": She is still here.
  • After the first auxiliary: She has still not replied.

In negative sentences, "still" often emphasizes that something expected has not happened.

I still haven't received the results.

5. STILL versus YET

These words can both appear in negative sentences, but their emphasis and position differ.

Still: She still hasn't finished.

The situation continues, perhaps longer than expected.

Yet: She hasn't finished yet.

The action has not happened up to now, but it may happen.

"Yet" commonly appears at the end of negative sentences and questions:

  • I haven't called him yet.
  • Have you completed the exercise yet?

Compare with "still", which normally appears before the main verb or after an auxiliary:

  • I am still waiting.
  • He still hasn't answered.
